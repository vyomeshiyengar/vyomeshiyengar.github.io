What to look for when evaluating AI tools for your team

There are hundreds of AI tools out there right now. For a small or medium business, the question is not, "Which AI tool is the most advanced?", the better question is, "What is the workflow I am looking to make better, and which tool can do that without creating more confusion?".

Start with the job, not the tool.

Do you want to summarize meetings? Draft client emails? Search internal documents? Create reports? Move information between systems? Help the team answer common questions? Those are different problems.

A general AI tool like ChatGPT or Claude can be very useful for writing, summarizing, planning, and analyzing information. A meeting tool like Fireflies or Fathom can help with transcripts, summaries, and action items. A tool like n8n or Make can connect apps so information moves automatically. The right tool depends on the work.

The next thing to look at is whether the tool fits into what your team already uses. If your team lives in Google Drive, Slack, Gmail, Microsoft 365, Notion, or a CRM, the tool should ideally connect to those places. AI is only really helpful when it has the right context and can use the data to learn about your workflows. You should care less about the longest feature list and more about whether the tool fits your actual setup.

As with any decision you make, the people need to be considered. The best AI tool is not always the one with the most features. It is the one your team will actually use. If a tool requires everyone to change how they work, adoption will be harder. If it fits into an existing process and saves time quickly, people are much more likely to stick with it. This is why I usually recommend starting with one focused use case instead of buying a big platform right away. Pick one problem, test one tool, see whether it creates real value.

Arguably one of the most important considerations, is security and permissions. Can you manage users? Can you control what data is connected? Can you decide who has access to what? Can your team review AI outputs before anything goes to a client? These questions are not just for big companies. They matter for SMBs too, especially if you handle client information, financial records, legal documents, healthcare information, or anything sensitive. A tool can be powerful and still be the wrong fit if you do not have enough control.

The best way to evaluate a tool is to test it with real work.

Take one recent client call, proposal, report, intake form, or spreadsheet. Try the tool on that. Then ask:

  • Did it save time?
  • Was the output actually useful?
  • Did someone still need to redo most of it?
  • Would the team trust this after a few tries?
  • Did it fit naturally into the work?

If the answer is mostly no, the tool is probably not worth paying for yet.

A lot of businesses end up with 5 different AI subscriptions and no clear system. One for writing, one for notes, one for search, one for automation, and one someone tried for 2 weeks and forgot about. Before adding another tool, ask what it replaces or improves. If it does not save time, reduce manual work, or help your team make better decisions, it may just be noise. The goal is to make your business easier to run.

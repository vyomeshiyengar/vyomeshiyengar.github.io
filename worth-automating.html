How to figure out what's worth automating (and what isn't)

Not everything should be automated. That might sound strange coming from someone who helps businesses use AI, but it is true. Some tasks are perfect for automation while others need too much judgment, context, or relationship awareness to hand off fully.

The goal is to figure out where technology can save real time without making your business harder to run. The best tasks to automate usually have 3 things in common.

They happen often, they follow a similar pattern, and the output is easy to review.

Think about things like sending follow-up emails after calls, setting up appointments, turning intake forms into summaries, pulling details from invoices, creating monthly reports, routing requests, or drafting proposals from templates. These are good candidates because your team already knows what "good" looks like. That makes it easier to teach an AI tool what to do and easier for a person to check the result.

If a task happens once a year, it probably is not worth automating. If it happens 10 times a week, it is worth looking at. A lot of service businesses lose time because information gets moved around manually.

However, there are also tasks where you should be careful how you use AI. If something involves a sensitive client relationship, legal or financial risk, or a decision that depends on experience, AI should support the person doing the work, not replace them. A law firm can use AI to summarize intake notes, but a lawyer should review the matter. A bookkeeping firm can use AI to categorize expenses, but uncertain items should be checked. A recruiting agency can use AI to compare resumes against a role, but a recruiter should still make the final call.

A really useful setup is often "AI does the first pass, your team reviews." That saves time while keeping judgment where it belongs. When deciding whether something is worth automating, ask 4 simple questions:

  • Does this happen often?
  • Does it follow a predictable pattern?
  • Would saving time here actually matter?
  • Can a person review the result before it affects a client?

If the answer is yes to most of those, it is probably worth exploring. If the task is rare, unclear, risky, or deeply personal, it may be better left alone for now.

The best place to start is usually smaller than people think. Pick one annoying task that everyone understands. Something small enough to test, but meaningful enough that the team would notice if it got easier. That might be meeting summaries. It might be client follow-ups. It might be turning messy notes into proposal drafts. Once your team sees one practical example, the next opportunities become much easier to spot.
